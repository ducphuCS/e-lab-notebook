## 1. Why now

The ELN is our **electronic lab notebook** — one place where experiments, teams, and knowledge connect. It is built *with* the people who work in the lab, *for* the people who work in the lab.

Three things have changed:

| | What we see today | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge is scattered** | Results live in notebooks, inboxes, and memory. | When someone leaves, part of the knowledge leaves with them. |
| **Handoffs are slow** | Formulators and the phys-chem lab re-explain context instead of reusing it. | Every handoff loses a little detail. |
| **Decisions need evidence** | Scale-up, audits, and smarter experiments depend on traceable data. | We need proof we can point to — not "who remembers what". |

> **Why now:** not because anyone failed — but because our current tools can't keep up with how much good work we do.

## 2. The problem we all feel

These are the days we've all had:

1. **"Where is that result?"** — A test is finished in the phys-chem lab, but the formulator finds out three days later — or never. Results are scattered by default.
2. **Five versions of the truth** — The same formula exists in a notebook, a spreadsheet, and an old file. When they disagree, nobody knows which one is real.
3. **Handover means re-explaining** — Every new project member starts by digging through emails and asking around. Onboarding is archaeology.
4. **The memory is in people's heads** — We know more than we can show. If an experiment isn't recorded, it doesn't compound into the next one.
5. **Repeated work** — Batches are re-made and tests re-run because the first record was incomplete. Duplication costs days, every quarter.

> **The ELN is our answer to this.** And it only works if it's designed around your real days in the lab — which is exactly why we're doing this together.

## 3. “Is this better than Excel?”

> *When we first brought up the ELN, the first question from the formulators was:*
> **“Is this better than just using Excel files?”**
>
> *It's the right question to ask. Here is our honest answer.*

Excel has served us well. It's flexible, everyone knows it, and it's already on every desk. The question isn't whether Excel is bad — it's whether it can carry us from here.

| | Excel today | ELN |
|---|---|---|
| **Where the data lives** | In files on laptops, inboxes, and shared drives — whoever has the file has the data | One shared home for all R&D work |
| **Versions of the truth** | Copies drift; the same formula exists in five files with five answers | One source of truth, versioned and trusted |
| **Experiment timeline** | Tracked manually, if at all; reconstructed from memory | Every batch has a clear, searchable timeline — plan → batch → samples → reports |
| **Handoffs** | Formulator sends a file; the phys-chem lab re-asks for context | Teams work on the same shared data; context travels with it |
| **Formulation calculations** | Re-built formula by formula, file by file | Calculation tools built into the Library |
| **Learnings from experiments** | Live in people's heads and old files | DoE chains in Analyze — learnings compound |
| **The daily routine** | Many places to look, many habits to keep | One daily habit, one home |

**What Excel is genuinely good at** — and we're not asking you to give it up overnight:

- Quick lists and one-off calculations, exactly the way you like them.
- Personal notes and scratch work — private, flexible, yours.
- It's already there, and you're already good at it.

**Where Excel can't carry us** — no matter how carefully we use it:

- It has no single source of truth: copies multiply by design.
- It doesn't connect teams: a file sent is a conversation that ends.
- It doesn't remember for us: timelines, the links between batches and samples, and DoE chains all depend on discipline and memory.

> **Our promise:** the ELN has to be *better than your Excel file* — for you, in your day. If it isn't, we haven't finished building it. So bring the workbook that works for you; we'll treat it as the spec.

## 4. One experiment, told end to end

Traceability isn't an abstract feature. It's what happens when a single batch leaves a trace that anyone can follow — from idea to insight.

| Step | Who | What happens | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **Design the formula** | Formulator | Opens the formula in the Library; the calculation tool re-sizes it for the target batch. | Library |
| 2. **Run the batch** | Formulator | The batch is recorded as it happens: actual yield, observations, deviations. | Lab |
| 3. **Take the samples** | Formulator | Samples are taken and linked to the batch — they carry its identity everywhere they go. | Lab |
| 4. **Run the tests** | Phys-chem lab | Pulls the standard panel from the Library and attaches reports to each sample. | Lab · Library |
| 5. **Learn from the chain** | R&D team | Analyze links this batch to earlier DoEs — what changed, what mattered most. | Analyze |
| 6. **Decide the next step** | Everyone | The learning shapes the next experiment. The loop closes — nothing is lost between rounds. | The loop |

## 5. The four modules

The ELN is **four connected groups of functions** — each one maps to a real moment in your work.

| Module | One-line purpose | A day in the lab |
|---|---|---|
| **01 · Overview** | See the whole picture at a glance — projects, timelines, risks, and workload. | Monday morning you open Overview: four batches on the timeline, phys-chem tests still pending for batch 2, and a risk flag on an ingredient that just went out of stock. You get the picture without a single status meeting. |
| **02 · Library** | Prepare once — ingredients, equipment, methods, panels, formulas — reuse always. | Before starting a batch, the formulator picks the formula; the calculation tool recalculates the recipe for the target batch size. The phys-chem lab pulls the standard test panel from the same place — one version, no drift. |
| **03 · Lab** | Record what actually happened — not what was planned. | The formulator records actual yield, takes three samples, and links them to the batch. The phys-chem lab uploads test reports directly to each sample. Months later, anyone can open batch B-0142 and read its full story. |
| **04 · Analyze** | Learn from chains of experiments — and design the next ones on top of that learning. | Three DoEs on emulsion stability are linked into a chain: what changed between them, and which factor mattered most. The next DoE is designed on top of that learning — not from scratch. |

### 4.1 Overview

Focuses on the **timeline of projects, risks, and individual workload**.

- **Formulators** — know where your project stands without chasing emails.
- **Phys-chem lab** — see upcoming test load and plan capacity fairly.
- **R&D leads** — spot risks and bottlenecks before they become delays.

### 4.2 Library

Contains the **pre-prepared information before the actual lab work**: ingredients, equipment, test methods, test panels, formulas.

- **Formulators** — formulas and their calculations in one trusted place.
- **Phys-chem lab** — test methods and panels are always the current version.
- **All teams** — one vocabulary: same ingredient codes, same units, same names.

### 4.3 Lab

Records **what actually happened**: actual yield, samples from the batch, and test reports for each sample.

- **Formulators** — every batch documented as it happens. No memory gaps.
- **Phys-chem lab** — results attach to the right sample, automatically.
- **R&D** — a trustworthy record for audits, scale-up, and handover.

### 4.4 Analyze

Shows the **analytical side of the lab**: learnings from chains of DoEs, and designing new DoEs.

- **Formulators** — decide the next experiment with evidence, not gut feel.
- **R&D** — learnings compound across projects, not just inside one person.
- **All teams** — every experiment becomes input for the next one.

## 6. Core values

Five commitments shape every design decision we make:

| Value | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **One daily habit** | The ELN becomes part of everyone's routine — the default place to open, record, and check. Not an extra task on top of work; the place work happens. |
| **Traceability** | Every experiment has a clear, searchable timeline — from plan, to batch, to sample, to test report. Anyone can retrace any result. |
| **Collaboration** | Formulators, the phys-chem lab, and other teams work on shared data — not forwarded files. Handoffs become hand-ons. |
| **Data integrity** | One single source of truth — versioned, protected, and trusted by every team. What the ELN says is what the company knows. |
| **Built-in intelligence** | Formulation calculation tools and DoE-driven analysis turn recorded data into decisions. |

## 7. Open question: should the ELN track individual workload?

We're putting this on the table honestly. Tracking who does what **could** help planning — and it **could** also feel like surveillance. **This is a conversation for the teams, not a settled feature.**

| What it could help with | What we must handle carefully |
|---|---|
| See where bottlenecks really are — and why work queues up | It can feel like surveillance if it isn't designed with the teams |
| Plan experiments and tests more fairly across teams | People may optimise the metric instead of the science |
| Balance load before people burn out, not after | Lab, analysis, and desk work are hard to compare fairly |
| Make contribution visible — and recognisable | Trust is easy to break and very hard to repair |

**Questions to bring to your team:**

1. What would make workload tracking feel **useful** — not **watched**?
2. Team-level visibility or individual? What feels fair in *your* role?
3. Which signals actually reflect good work in your function — and which ones would distort it?

> **Our commitment:** if we ever build this, it will be because the teams asked for it — and designed it themselves. Until then, it stays an open question.

## 8. How you can contribute

The ELN is built from real work, not from a slide deck. Here's how each team can shape it — concretely.

| Team | Your concrete contribution |
|---|---|
| **Formulators** | Bring your real formula templates and your calculation pain points. Tell us which calculation would save you an hour a week — that's the feature we build first. → *Pilot the formula module with a real recipe — and bring the Excel workbook that works for you; it becomes the spec.* |
| **Phys-chem lab** | Define the test methods and panels that go into the Library — your expertise becomes the shared standard everyone else relies on. → *Pilot capturing test reports in the Lab module.* |
| **R&D & project leads** | Shape the Overview: what timeline, risk, and capacity views would actually help you plan — and which reporting should just go away? → *Define the project & risk views with us.* |
| **Everyone in R&D** | Adopt the daily habit early and help it stick. Share your worst "where-is-the-result" day — those stories become the requirements. → *Join a working-group session.* |
